Tankless Water Heater for Radiant Floor Heat in Burnsville, MN.

Radiant floor heat and tankless heaters both save space, so people ask whether one unit can do both jobs. The answer depends on how the system is built.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026
.

.

The short answer.

A tankless heater can run radiant floor heat in some designs, but a standard tankless unit installed only for domestic hot water is not set up for it. Radiant systems need a heater rated for space heating, correct controls and a way to keep heating water separate from drinking water.

In a cold climate this is a design question, not a swap. Get it wrong and you can get cold floors, a damaged heater or contaminated hot water.

Open loop and closed loop.

System.How it works.Considerations.
Open loop.Potable water flows through the floor tubing and then out the taps.Few approved setups, risk of stagnant water and scale.
Closed loop with heat exchanger.Heater warms a separate loop, which carries heat to the floor.Separates heating water from drinking water, the usual choice.
Dedicated boiler.A boiler heats the loop, and an indirect tank handles hot water.Common and well understood in cold climates.

Closed loop designs are the standard approach. A heat exchanger transfers heat from the heater to a sealed loop that can hold antifreeze. The floor loop never mixes with the water you drink.

Sizing is the hard part.

A tankless heater is sized for flow and temperature rise for showers and taps. Floor heating is a different load, spread over many hours and sized to the heat loss of the house. A tankless unit that runs the floors may have no capacity left when someone takes a shower.

Some designs use a tankless unit built for combined space and water heating. Others use a separate boiler for the floor loop. Make sure the manufacturer approves the unit for the purpose. Our tankless water heater page covers sizing and venting in general.

What a Minnesota winter asks of the system.

Cold-weather capacity.

The system must hold temperature on the coldest days, not average ones.

Freeze protection.

A closed loop can use antifreeze, but the right type and concentration matter.

Pipe layout.

Loop length and spacing affect how warm the floor feels.

Controls.

Zone controls, mixing valves and thermostats make the system comfortable.

Hard water and the heat exchanger.

Scale is the enemy of any tankless heater. If the unit also serves the floor loop through a heat exchanger, scale on the domestic side cuts efficiency and raises operating cost. A softener and a regular flush are part of the plan, not extras.

See water softener installation for how we set one up for a heater.

When a boiler is the better answer.

For a whole-house radiant system, a boiler is built for the job. It handles the steady load of floor heating and can feed an indirect water heater for domestic use. See boiler repair if you already have one.

A tankless unit with a heat exchanger is a fit for a small zone, a garage floor or a bathroom. For a large radiant system, ask an engineer or a heating contractor to size it.

Questions to ask a designer or installer.

Ask for the heat loss calculation, the required output in BTUs and the flow rate the system needs. These three numbers decide whether a given heater can do the job. If the installer cannot produce them, the system has not been designed.

Ask who maintains the system and what the annual service includes. A combined system has more parts than a simple heater, and each part needs attention. Ask what happens in a power outage, since many tankless units and circulator pumps stop without electricity.

Control and zoning.

A radiant system works best when each zone has its own thermostat and a mixing valve or injection control that keeps the floor loop at a safe temperature. Floor temperatures that are too high are uncomfortable and can damage flooring.

Zoning lets you keep a bedroom cooler than a living room. Ask the designer how many zones the system has, what the controls do and whether they can be reset after a power outage.

A final tip: ask whether the design allows for service. A system buried in a slab with no access to the heat exchanger or pumps is hard to maintain. Good designs put the mechanical parts where a plumber can reach them.

Burnsville facts that matter for this setup.

The City of Burnsville's 2025 drinking water report lists hardness at 25 to 26 grains per gallon. Any heater that heats Burnsville tap water will scale, so plan a softener and a yearly flush.

The City of Burnsville lists water heaters among the projects that need a plumbing permit. Call Building Inspections at 952-895-4444 to ask how a combined heating and water system is handled.

Gas piping, venting and heating loops fall under different trades. A licensed plumber can verify the plumbing side. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry offers a Check a License tool.
