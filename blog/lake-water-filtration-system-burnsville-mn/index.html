Lake Water Filtration System for Burnsville, MN Homes.

Searches for a lake water filtration system usually come from people near a lake who wonder what they should be filtering. For most Burnsville homes the answer starts with the city water report.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026

Where your tap water actually comes from.

Burnsville has several lakes, including Crystal Lake and Keller Lake, but the taps in Burnsville homes do not run on lake water. The City of Burnsville supplies groundwater that it treats and tests, and publishes the results each year.

That changes the filtration question. A house on city water is not filtering lake water at all. It is deciding whether anything needs to be removed from treated groundwater, and the answer is mostly hardness.

What a house on city water may want.

Concern.Treatment.Notes.
Hardness (25 to 26 grains per gallon).Water softener.Protects heater, fixtures and appliances.
Taste or odor.Carbon filter at the sink or whole house.Test first so you know what you are removing.
Sediment after main work.Sediment pre-filter.Catches grit and particles.
Lead in old fixtures or solder.Certified point-of-use filter.Check what the water report says.

A water softener is the filter that addresses what Burnsville water actually has. A whole-house carbon filter or a sediment filter is optional and depends on a specific complaint.

If you draw lake water for the yard.

Some homeowners on a lake pump lake water to irrigate. That water is not treated, and it can carry sediment, algae and organic material. It is not for drinking and it should never be connected to the house plumbing.

A sediment filter ahead of a sprinkler pump prevents clogged heads. Check local rules before you pump from a lake, and ask the city and the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources about permits.

Never connect it to the house.

A cross-connection between lake water and the potable system is a health risk.

Use a strainer.

Weeds and silt clog irrigation pumps and heads.

Protect against backflow.

Irrigation lines tied to city water need a backflow preventer.

Do not drink it.

Untreated lake water is not safe to drink without proper treatment.

How to pick a filter honestly.

Read the water report.

It lists what the city tests for and the results.

Test your tap.

A home test or lab test shows what is in your water.

Match the filter to the problem.

Do not buy a system for a contaminant you do not have.

Plan the maintenance.

Filters and softener salt cost money over time.

Many marketing claims about whole-house systems go beyond what the water report supports. Ask any salesperson to show the test result they are solving. A plumber can install the system and connect it correctly, but you should know what it is for.

Installing a whole-house system.

A whole-house filter or softener installs where the main water line enters the house, after the meter and the main shutoff. It needs bypass valves, space and a drain for backwash or brine. See plumbing installation for the plumbing side.

Questions to ask before you buy any system.

Ask what the system removes and which test result it answers. A salesperson who cannot name the problem it solves is selling a feeling. Ask what the maintenance costs each year, including filters, salt and any service visits.

Ask who installs it and whether the installer is licensed. A system that connects to the main water line is plumbing work, and a bad connection can leak or cross-connect with other supplies.

A fair order of operations.

  • Read the city water report for your area.
  • Test your own tap for hardness and any specific complaint.
  • Choose the smallest system that solves the measured problem.
  • Install it with bypass valves so you can service it.
  • Set a calendar reminder for filters and salt.

Taste, smell and the filters that fix them.

A carbon filter is the usual answer to a chlorine taste or a faint odor. It works at the sink or on the whole house, and the cartridge has to be replaced on schedule or it stops working and can harbor bacteria. A sink-side filter treats only the water you drink and cook with, which is where most people notice taste.

A sediment filter catches particles and is worth considering after street work or main repairs stir up debris. Neither of these filters softens water, so they sit alongside a softener, not in place of one.

What the City of Burnsville reports.

The City of Burnsville's 2025 drinking water report lists hardness at 25 to 26 grains per gallon. It also documents the water sources and the results of the tests the city runs on the supply.

The city lists water softeners among the projects handled through its plumbing permit process. Building Inspections can be reached at 952-895-4444 to confirm what applies to a filter or softener installation.

If you draw water from a lake, ask the City of Burnsville and the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources what rules apply before you install a pump.

Any plumbing connected to the house must meet the Minnesota Plumbing Code, the state standard in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, including protection against cross-connection with other water sources.
