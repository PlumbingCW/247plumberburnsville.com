Types of Plumbing Pipes in Burnsville, MN Homes.

Pipe material decides how a house leaks, how it freezes and when it needs replacement. This guide helps a Burnsville homeowner recognize each type and understand what it means.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026
.

Supply pipes and drain pipes are different systems.

Supply pipes carry pressurized clean water to fixtures. Drain pipes carry waste away by gravity. They use different materials, and the failure signs differ.

A leak on a supply line shows up as a drip or a drop in pressure. A drain problem shows up as slow drains or backups.

Look at the pipes in your basement or utility room first. Most of the material you need to identify is visible there.

Common supply materials.

Material.How to recognize it.Good to know.
Copper.Reddish brown or green with age, rigid, soldered joints.Long record, can pit or split when frozen.
PEX.Flexible plastic, often red, blue or white.Tolerates freezing better, needs the right fittings.
CPVC.Cream or light yellow, rigid plastic.Can turn brittle with age.
Galvanized steel.Gray, threaded fittings, rusty at joints.Corrodes from the inside and restricts flow.

Galvanized steel is the one to watch in older houses. It narrows over time as rust builds inside, and pressure drops. If your home has it, a full or partial repiping is likely in your future.

Common drain materials.

Material.How to recognize it.Good to know.
PVC and ABS.White or black plastic, glued joints.Common in newer work, light and easy to join.
Cast iron.Heavy, dark, rough surface, bell-and-spigot joints.Long life, can rust and scale inside.
Clay.Buried, tan, short sections.Roots enter at joints.

Cast iron and clay are the materials most often found in older sewer and drain lines. A camera inspection shows what is inside. See sewer camera inspection to understand what it finds.

How each type fails.

Copper.

Pinholes, green stains and splits where water froze.

PEX.

Failures usually occur at fittings, not the pipe.

Galvanized.

Falling pressure, rusty water and leaks at threaded joints.

Cast iron.

Scale and slow drains, and in time cracks or holes.

Freezing and Minnesota winters.

Water expands when it freezes, and rigid pipe can split. Copper in an exterior wall or an unheated space is the usual victim. PEX can expand a little without splitting, but it is not freezeproof.

If a pipe in your house has frozen once, it will freeze again. See burst pipe repair for what to do in the moment, and consider moving or insulating the run.

How to read what you find.

Walk the basement with a flashlight and look at three places: where the water line enters the house, the pipes leaving the water heater and the drain lines under the main bathroom. These three spots usually show every material in the house.

Do not judge a pipe by its color alone, because paint and insulation hide the truth. A magnet sticks to galvanized steel but not to copper or plastic. A scratch with a coin on the surface of an old supply pipe shows bright copper or gray steel underneath.

What to write down.

Note the material at the main shutoff, the water heater and each bathroom. A simple list helps any plumber who visits later, and it helps you decide whether a repair is a one-time fix or a sign of age.

What mixed materials mean.

Many houses have more than one supply material, because earlier owners repaired sections with whatever was handy. A mix of copper and galvanized pipe can corrode at the joint where they meet unless a dielectric fitting separates them. A leak at such a joint tells you about the connection, not the whole run.

Ask a plumber to look at each transition. A new PEX section connected to old copper needs the right fittings. A repair that uses the wrong adapter will fail early and may void part of a warranty.

If you find pipe you cannot identify, take a clear photo and share it with the plumber on the phone. Many materials are recognizable from a picture, and it saves guesswork before a visit.

Never cut into an old pipe you cannot identify until you know what it is. Some old materials need special handling.

Burnsville water and the pipes in your house.

The City of Burnsville's 2025 drinking water report lists hardness at 25 to 26 grains per gallon. Hard water leaves scale inside pipes of any material, which reduces flow over time.

The same report lists 17,004 non-lead service lines and none requiring replacement for lead or galvanized steel. That covers the city side of the connection. The pipes inside your house are your own, and they are the ones worth identifying.

Minnesota Rules chapter 4714 sets which materials are allowed for which uses. A licensed plumber knows what applies, and the Department of Labor and Industry offers a Check a License tool.

The Minnesota Plumbing Code is the state standard for which pipe materials may be used in which places.
