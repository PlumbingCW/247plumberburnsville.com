Replacing a Water Heater Igniter in Burnsville, MN.

If your gas water heater clicks, glows or tries to light and then goes cold, the igniter is a prime suspect. This guide explains how the part works, what a safe test looks like and where a homeowner should stop.

What an igniter does.

Most gas water heaters built in the last twenty years do not keep a pilot burning all day. Instead a small igniter lights the burner when the thermostat calls for heat. The igniter is either a hot surface type that glows orange or a spark type that clicks.

A weak or cracked igniter fails to light the gas. The control board then shuts the gas off for safety, and you are left with cold water and a heater that looks fine from the outside.

Signs the igniter is the problem.

Clicking with no flame.

A spark igniter that clicks but never lights the burner may have a worn electrode or a gas supply problem.

No glow at all.

A hot surface igniter that stays dark is usually burned out or has lost power.

Lights, then dies.

A flame that starts and drops out points to the flame sensor or thermocouple, not the igniter.

Error code on the control.

Many heaters flash a code that names the igniter or the flame circuit.

Before you blame the igniter, check the simple things. Confirm the gas valve is open, the power switch is on and the thermostat is set above the tank temperature. A tripped breaker or a closed gas valve looks exactly like a failed part.

Safety first, because this is a gas appliance.

Stop and call if any of these apply.

  • You smell gas at any point. Leave the house and call the gas utility from outside..
  • The burner area shows soot, scorch marks or a yellow flame..
  • The vent pipe is loose, rusted through or warm to the touch outside the flue..
  • You cannot find the shutoff for the gas supply..

A gas leak is an emergency. Our line is open 24/7 for no-hot-water calls that involve a gas heater.

How to test the igniter.

Power down.

Turn the gas control to off and switch off power to the heater if it has an electrical connection.

Remove the access panel.

Take off the burner compartment cover and find the igniter near the burner tube.

Inspect.

Look for hairline cracks in a hot surface igniter or a worn tip on a spark electrode.

Test resistance.

A meter reading outside the range printed in the manual means the part should be replaced.

A cracked igniter is often visible, but a failed one can look perfect. If you do not own a meter or do not know how to read it, stop here. Replacing a part that was not the cause costs money and does not bring hot water back.

Replacing it.

The igniter is matched to the heater model, so the part number on the data plate matters. Buying the wrong igniter is the most common mistake. The new part usually slips into a bracket and connects with a plug.

Handle a hot surface igniter by the ceramic base only. Oil from your fingers can create a hot spot that shortens its life. After installation, restore power and gas, set the thermostat and watch the first ignition cycle from a safe distance.

When repair is the wrong answer.

An igniter is a cheap part on a heater that is otherwise sound. If the tank is old, rusted at the base or leaking, a new igniter only delays the bigger decision. Check the manufacture date on the data plate. A tank that is past its normal service life is a candidate for replacement.

Burnsville facts that affect your heater.

The City of Burnsville's 2025 drinking water report lists hardness at 25 to 26 grains per gallon. Minerals from hard water collect on the tank bottom and around the burner chamber, which can foul a burner and slow ignition. A yearly flush is worth the effort here.

The City of Burnsville lists water heaters among the projects that need a plumbing permit. A homeowner who owns and occupies the home may apply for it. Building Inspections can be reached at 952-895-4444 to confirm what applies before you start.

Minnesota licenses plumbers, and the Department of Labor and Industry offers a Check a License tool. Use it if you decide to hire out the work.

Gas water heater venting follows the state standard in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714 and the maker's instructions.
