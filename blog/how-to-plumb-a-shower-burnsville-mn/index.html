How to Plumb a Shower in Burnsville, MN.

A shower looks simple once it is finished, but the plumbing behind the wall must be right the first time. This guide explains the parts, the order of work and where a permit or a plumber comes in.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026

The five parts of a shower.

Supply lines.

Hot and cold lines run from the main supply to the shower valve.

Valve.

A pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve mixes hot and cold and protects against scalding.

Shower arm and head.

The arm comes out of the wall at the height you choose.

Drain and trap.

The trap holds a water seal that blocks sewer gas.

Vent.

The vent lets air into the drain so water flows freely.

Each part has to be in the right place before the walls close, because moving any of them afterward means opening the wall. That is why rough-in is done first and inspected before the cement board and tile go up.

Step by step.

Plan the layout.

Choose the valve height, the head location and the drain position, and check the framing.

Run the supply.

Hot and cold lines tie into the existing supply with shutoff valves nearby.

Set the valve.

The valve body is secured to blocking so it does not move.

Rough in the drain.

The drain connects to a trap and slopes toward the main drain at the required pitch.

Test and inspect.

The system is pressure tested, then inspected before walls are closed.

Why the drain is the tricky part.

The supply side is mostly small pipe. The drain side is where mistakes cost money.

A shower drain needs a minimum pipe size, a trap and a vent, and the slope must be consistent. Too flat and solids collect. Too steep and water outruns the solids.

A basement shower is harder, because the drain may sit below the sewer. In that case waste must be pumped up, which needs an ejector pit and a sealed, vented cover. Plan that before cutting the slab. See plumbing installation for how we handle basement rough-in.

Hot water capacity.

A new shower is an added load on the water heater. If the family already runs out of hot water during the morning rush, another shower makes it worse. Check the tank size and recovery rate before you commit. See water heater installation if a second heater or a larger unit is the answer.

Do it yourself or hire it out.

The valve and drain are the hard parts. Mistakes there show up as leaks inside a wall, slow drains or a shower that scalds. Many homeowners prepare the space and hire a plumber for the rough-in and the connection.

If you do the work yourself, test the lines under pressure and leave the walls open until the inspector signs off.

Mistakes that show up after the tile is on.

The costly errors in a shower are the ones hidden inside the wall. A valve set too deep or too shallow will not fit the trim kit. A drain with too little slope holds water. A supply joint that was never pressure tested leaks inside a finished wall for months before a stain shows.

Pressure test before you close the wall, and leave the test gauge in place until the inspector has seen it. Then test the shower pan with standing water for at least a day. These two tests catch almost every hidden leak.

Choosing a valve before the wall is framed.

The valve decides how the shower behaves. A pressure-balancing valve keeps the temperature steady when someone flushes a toilet or starts the washing machine. A thermostatic valve lets you set an exact temperature and often supports several outlets, such as a head and a hand shower.

Buy the valve and the trim kit together, and read the rough-in depth in the instructions. The valve body must sit at the depth the trim expects relative to the finished wall surface. A thick tile and backer board change that depth.

Permits and licensing in Burnsville.

The City of Burnsville's Plumbing Permit page explains when a plumbing permit is required. It lists specific projects, and Building Inspections at 952-895-4444 can confirm whether a new shower needs one. The city says a homeowner who owns and occupies the home may apply, and licensed plumbers pull permits for their own work.

The Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, sets the standards for pipe sizes, traps, venting and fixtures. A local inspector reviews work against it.

The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry licenses plumbers and offers a Check a License tool, which is the way to verify someone before you hire them.
