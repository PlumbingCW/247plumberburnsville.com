New Plumbing Installation in Burnsville, MN Homes.

New plumbing is a project with a sequence. The decisions you make before the work starts decide whether it goes smoothly.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026
.
Plumbing question?
.

The stages of a new plumbing job.

Plan.

Decide where the fixtures go, what they are and how they connect.

Permit.

File the permit if the project requires one.

Rough-in.

Run supply, drain and vent lines before walls close.

Inspect.

An inspector reviews the rough-in.

Finish.

Set fixtures, test and final inspection.

Skipping or reordering these stages is how projects end with tile that must be torn out. Rough-in comes first, inspection second and finish work last.

Planning decisions.

Fixture location drives everything. Moving a toilet a few feet may mean cutting the slab. A sink on an exterior wall may need extra protection against freezing. A kitchen island sink needs a special venting arrangement.

Decide the fixtures before the plumber starts. The rough-in dimensions depend on the exact toilet, shower valve or sink you choose.

Project.What to settle first.
New bathroom.Drain location, vent path, water heater capacity.
Kitchen remodel.Sink and dishwasher location, disposal, gas line if any.
Basement bath.Whether the drain is above or below the sewer, ejector pump.
Laundry.Washer box location, drain size, shutoff valves.
Outdoor spigot.Frost-proof design and backflow protection.

Check capacity first.

A new bathroom adds demand on the water heater and the supply pipe. If the existing pipe is old, a remodel is a good time to replace it. See repiping for when that makes sense.

Check the drain as well. A new fixture on an old, partly blocked drain will back up. A camera check of the main line before you add load is cheap insurance.

Basement installations.

A basement bath below the level of the sewer needs a sealed ejector pit and a pump. The pit must be vented and have a tight lid. The pump needs electrical power, and a power outage stops it.

Fix any water problems first. A new bathroom over a damp slab will not last. The sump pump page covers the groundwater side.

Write the scope.

List every fixture and every connection.

Get the price in writing.

A written price avoids surprises.

Ask who pulls the permit.

Permits protect you at resale.

Plan the patching.

Someone has to repair walls and floors after the plumbing.

Choosing who does the work.

A licensed plumber knows the code and the inspection process. Homeowners who do their own work should know the rough-in requirements and schedule the inspection before closing walls.

Compare written scopes, not just prices. A low price that leaves out the permit or the patching is not a low price.

Questions that save money later.

Ask whether the existing supply pipe can feed the new fixtures at good pressure. A new bath on old half-inch galvanized pipe may leave every fixture in the house with weak flow. Ask whether the main drain can take the added load without backing up.

Ask for the inspection schedule up front. A project that waits two days for an inspector with open walls stays on schedule. A project that closed its walls early has to open them again.

What a good written quote includes.

A written quote for new plumbing should name each fixture, each connection and each pipe run. It should say who supplies the fixtures, who pulls the permit and who schedules the inspection. It should say what is not included, such as drywall repair, tile and painting.

Compare the scope of two quotes before you compare the numbers. A price that leaves out the permit or the patching is not a lower price. It is a different job.

Quote item.Why it matters.
Fixtures and models.The rough-in depends on the exact fixture.
Permit and inspection.Unpermitted work can cause trouble at resale.
Access and patching.Opening walls and closing them is real work.
Timeline.Rough-in must be inspected before walls close.
Warranty.Ask what is covered and for how long.

If the job includes a new gas line or any electrical work, ask who does that part. A plumber handles water, drain and vent, and may handle gas piping. Electrical work belongs to an electrician.

Finally, plan the order of trades. Plumbing rough-in comes before electrical in some walls and after it in others, and drywall comes only after inspections. A project manager who knows the order saves days of waiting, and a homeowner who asks about it early avoids paying for a second visit.

Permits, code and licensing.

The City of Burnsville's Plumbing Permit page explains when a plumbing permit is required, and Building Inspections at 952-895-4444 answers questions about specific projects. The city says a homeowner who owns and occupies the home may apply for a permit.

The Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, governs plumbing work in the state. A local inspector reviews permitted work against it.

Minnesota licenses plumbers. The Department of Labor and Industry's Check a License tool lets you verify a plumber before you sign.

The Minnesota Plumbing Code is the state standard that inspectors use when they review new plumbing work.
