Sewage Sump Pump Installation in Burnsville, MN.

Sewage sump pumps are different machines from the groundwater pumps people usually mean. This guide explains the difference and what a proper installation includes.

By the Burnsville 24/7 Plumbing team | Published November 16, 2026

Sump pump or sewage pump?.

Type.Job.Handles solids?.
Sump pump.Removes groundwater from a pit.No, only clear or lightly dirty water.
Sewage ejector pump.Lifts waste from a basement bath to the sewer line.Yes, designed for solids.
Grinder pump.Grinds and pumps waste under pressure.Yes, for long or high lifts.

A basement toilet, shower or laundry sink that sits below the level of the sewer cannot drain by gravity. Its waste goes into a sealed pit, and an ejector pump lifts it to the main line. Using a standard sump pump for this job fails quickly.

Parts of a sewage ejector system.

Sealed basin.

A pit with a gasketed lid keeps sewer gas out of the house.

Pump.

A pump rated for sewage handles solids and paper.

Check valve.

It stops waste from flowing back into the pit.

Vent.

The basin needs its own vent to the roof or a code-approved connection.

Alarm.

A high water alarm warns before the pit overflows.

What installation involves.

Break the slab.

A section of floor is cut and removed to place the basin.

Set the basin.

The pit is set level and the drain lines are connected.

Connect the pump.

Discharge piping runs to the sewer line with a check valve and a shutoff.

Seal and test.

The lid is sealed, the vent is connected and the system is tested with water.

Electrical power matters. A pump needs a dedicated circuit, and a power outage stops it. A basement bath with an ejector should have a plan for outages, such as a backup power option or a habit of not using it during a blackout.

Common problems.

Wipes and feminine products jam sewage pumps. Grease and hair collect in the pit. A float switch that sticks keeps the pump from starting. A failed check valve lets waste flow back.

Most of these problems are preventable. Keep non-flushables out, test the pump twice a year and replace the check valve if it clatters. If the pump has failed, see sump pump repair, which covers the groundwater side and the common pump faults.

Planning a basement bath.

A basement bath is a plumbing project with a large hidden part. Settle the drain depth and the pump before you design the room. Check the water first, since a damp slab and a sewage pit do not mix. See plumbing installation for how we plan these jobs and basement waterproofing for water problems.

What to ask before the slab is cut.

Ask how deep the basin goes and where the discharge line ties in. Ask about the vent route and whether it needs a new roof penetration. Ask what kind of pump and how many gallons it moves per minute at the lift height you need.

Ask for an alarm. A high water alarm costs little and gives you a warning before sewage reaches the floor. Ask where the pump and the basin will be easiest to service, because a pit buried behind finished walls is a headache when the pump needs replacing.

Maintenance for a sewage ejector.

Lift the basin lid twice a year and look at the pit with a flashlight. Check that the float moves freely and that the pump runs when you raise it by hand. Listen for unusual noises. A pump that sounds different from last time is telling you something.

Keep the lid sealed after every check. An unsealed basin lets sewer gas into the basement. Replace the lid gasket if it hardens or cracks, and test the alarm at the same time.

Warning signs.

A bathroom that smells after a flush, a toilet that drains slowly or a pump that runs without clearing the pit are all reasons to call. A pump that cycles every few minutes often has a failed check valve letting water fall back into the basin.

Check the warranty terms on the pump and the basin. Many sewage pumps carry a warranty that depends on correct installation, so ask the installer to document the work with photos and the model numbers.

Also ask about service access. A basin lid that sits under a finished floor or a built-in cabinet is a poor choice, because the pump will need attention at some point.

Permits and sewer rules in Burnsville.

The City of Burnsville's Plumbing Permit page explains when a plumbing permit is required. A sewage ejector installation involves drain, vent and pump work, so ask Building Inspections at 952-895-4444 how it is handled.

The Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714, sets the standards for sewage ejectors, basins, vents and check valves.

For a sewer backup, the City of Burnsville asks residents to call Public Works at 952-895-4550 on weekdays and 9-1-1 evenings and weekends. The Department of Labor and Industry offers a Check a License tool for plumbers.

We answer the emergency line 24/7 from Burnsville and send plumbers to Savage and Rosemount as well, so the same advice applies if you live just outside the city. If the job turns out to be bigger than a homeowner fix, call and describe what you see.
