Leak detection is the work of finding water that is going where it should not, before it damages a house. If you can see water spreading, close the main water valve first, then call. Our line runs 24/7 and active leaks are dispatched same day, including evenings and weekends.

Most callers do not begin with the word leak. They start with a symptom: the bill went up, the meter keeps moving, a ceiling stained, a floor feels warm, the sound of running water at night. Each of those has a different search path, and that is how this page is organized.

Start with your meter.

The City of Burnsville says every home has a water meter, usually near the laundry room or furnace, and that a digital meter that moves while no water is running may indicate a leak. The city also lists toilets and water softeners as common leak sources. That gives you a free first test.

Turn off every fixture and appliance, then watch the meter for several minutes. If the number or the low-flow indicator moves, water is leaving the system. Next, close the main shutoff and check again.

If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the house, on the service line.

High water bill, no visible leak.

A silent toilet flapper that leaks into the bowl can waste hundreds of gallons in a day without a sound. A leak in the irrigation line may show only as unusually green lawn. A pinhole in a copper line inside a wall can go unnoticed for months.

We test supply lines with pressure checks, listen for the sound of escaping water with acoustic equipment, and use thermal imaging to find warm spots on a floor or ceiling. For hot water slab or ceiling leaks we may also isolate the water heater supply to separate hot from cold. If the cause is a toilet, our toilet repair page covers the repair.

Ceiling, wall and under-floor leaks.

Ceiling stain.

A brown ring below a bathroom or above a supply line usually has an upstairs plumbing source.

Wall dampness.

Peeling paint or a musty smell behind a wall often points to a failing joint or supply line.

Floor warmth or swelling.

A warm patch can mean a hot water line is leaking in or under the floor.

Crawlspace or basement dampness.

Water on the lowest level may come from a supply leak, a drain leak or groundwater, and the source matters for the fix.

Slab leaks.

A slab leak is a leak in a pipe that runs under or inside a concrete floor. The signs are a warm spot on the floor, the sound of running water when nothing is on, damp flooring and a bill that keeps climbing. A hot water line that leaks under a slab often shows up as a warm patch you can feel with a bare foot.

We confirm it with a meter test, then pinpoint it with acoustic and thermal tools before anyone cuts concrete. The fix is usually a small opening over the leak, or a reroute of the line through the walls or ceiling so the slab stays intact. See repiping if the pipe under the floor has failed more than once.

Buried lines and yard leaks.

A leak on the service line between the meter and the house shows as a soggy strip of lawn, lost pressure or a meter that runs with the house shut off. A locator and acoustic gear trace the line and listen for the leak.

The City of Burnsville's 2025 water report lists 17,004 non-lead service lines and none requiring replacement for lead or galvanized steel. That describes the pipe material and not where a leak sits, so the meter test still comes first. Ask Public Works at 952-895-4550 where the city's responsibility for the line ends.

When the leak is on the service line, see our water line repair page for repair options. Sprinkler lines leak as well, and we can isolate them from the house supply to confirm.

Testing standards for supply lines.

Pressure testing of supply lines follows the practice described in the Minnesota Plumbing Code, Minnesota Rules chapter 4714. We use meter tests first because they cost nothing and confirm whether a leak exists before any equipment is used.
